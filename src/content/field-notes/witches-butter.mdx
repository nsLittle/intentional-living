text: |
  Witch’s Butter is a bright, jelly-like fungus that grows on fallen and decaying wood, often appearing suddenly after rain. Its golden-yellow to orange color stands out sharply against dark bark, making it easy to spot once you know what to look for.

  Instead of a cap or stem, it forms soft, lobed folds with a rubbery, gelatinous texture. It swells when wet and shrinks when dry, allowing it to persist through changing weather. Touching it reveals a cool, slippery surface — more like firm jelly than a typical mushroom.

  In the forest, Witch’s Butter feeds on dead wood, quietly helping break it down and return nutrients to the soil. Its presence often signals damp conditions and active decay, part of the slow, essential work of woodland renewal.

  Its strange appearance has long invited folklore. In European traditions, Witch’s Butter was linked to curses, spells, and protection magic. It was sometimes scraped away or pressed onto doorways and windowsills as a ward, believed to absorb ill intent or mark a protected boundary. In folk witchcraft, it’s associated with protection, thresholds, and visible warnings.

  While technically edible, it’s bland and rarely collected. For many foragers, Witch’s Butter is best left where it grows — a reminder that not all useful things need to be used.
